The bench

Syscomp CGR-201 ──► VCO tuning pin (Vt) ──► VCO (Crystek, Vcc 11.5 V) │ │ └─► scope CH1 └─► tee ──► spectrum analyzer (SSA3021X) └──► splitter ──► short cable ──► mixer ──► scope CH2 └────► +4.5 in cable ─┘

Three things under computer control: the Syscomp sets the tuning voltage, the analyzer reads the true frequency, the scope (SDS1104X-E) reads the tuning voltage and the mixer output. The mixer output is a DC voltage that depends on frequency. That is the phase detector.

Static calibration

Set a voltage, wait, read the frequency, read the mixer. Step up 0.02 V. Do that 176 times from 0 to 3.5 V. One sweep gives every plot in this section.

Plot 1

VCO: output frequency vs input voltage

What I did

For each voltage I nudged the Syscomp until scope CH1 read the target within 3 mV. Then I asked the analyzer for the peak twice: once wide to find it, once in a 2 MHz span to read it well.

Green dots go up. Black crosses are 14 points taken coming back down. They land on the curve within 0.10 MHz.

The slope is not constant: about 40 MHz/V below 1 V, 33 from 1 to 2 V, 75 from 2 to 3 V.

Math

Setting the voltage is a simple feedback loop on the Syscomp’s offset code, with \(k = 1.875\) mV per code:

\[ c_{n+1} = c_n + \frac{V_\text{target} - V_\text{CH1}}{k} \]

Marker resolution in the narrow span is \(2\ \text{MHz} / 751 \approx 2.7\) kHz.

Code: setting Vt and reading the peak 
def set_vt(g, s, target, code):
    s.write(f"C1:OFST {-target:.3f}V")      # keep CH1 centred on the target
    for _ in range(4):
        g.set_offset_raw(code)
        time.sleep(0.6)
        v, _ = scope_mean(s, 1, n=2)
        if abs(v - target) < 0.003:
            break
        code += (target - v) / OFFSET_V_PER_CODE
    return v, code

def measure(self):                          # spectrum analyzer
    f, _ = self._peak(850e6, 500e6)         # wide: find it
    f, p = self._peak(f, 2e6)               # narrow: read it
    return f, p
Plot 2

Splitter and mixer: output voltage vs input frequency

What I did

Same sweep. At each voltage I also read scope CH2, averaged over three 14 ms records. I plotted that against the frequency the analyzer gave, not against the voltage I set.

It rises cleanly up to 788 MHz, then turns over at 802, 834, 868 and 886 MHz. Above 788 MHz one voltage means several frequencies.

Math

An ideal delay-line detector gives a cosine in frequency, with \(\tau\) the extra delay of the longer cable:

\[ V_\text{out} \propto \cos(2\pi f \tau), \qquad \tau = \frac{\Delta L}{v} \approx \frac{4.5\ \text{in}}{0.7c} \approx 0.55\ \text{ns} \]

That predicts one slow swing over about 900 MHz. The measured curve wiggles every 30 to 50 MHz, so something else in the RF path is shaping it. I did not find out what.

Plot 3

The lookup: how mixer voltage becomes frequency

What I did

I kept only the 61 calibration points from 0 to 1.2 V, where the curve goes one way. I swapped the axes and drew straight lines between neighbours. A mixer voltage goes in, a frequency comes out.

The right panel is why it is not clean. The slope runs from 0.05 to 3.7 mV/MHz. Where it is shallow, below 755 MHz and above 785 MHz, a tiny voltage error is a big frequency error.

Point-to-point roughness of the table is 0.084 mV, about 40 kHz.

Math

Linear interpolation between the two calibration points that bracket the reading:

\[ f(V) = f_i + (V - V_i)\,\frac{f_{i+1} - f_i}{V_{i+1} - V_i}, \qquad V_i \le V \le V_{i+1} \]

A voltage error turns into a frequency error through the local slope:

\[ \delta f = \frac{\delta V}{dV/df} \]
Code: the lookup 
use = up[up["vt_meas_v"] <= 1.2]            # single-valued part only
cf, cv = use["vco_freq_hz"] / 1e6, use["mixer_out_v"] * 1e3
order = np.argsort(cv)

def mv_to_mhz(mv):
    return np.interp(mv, cv[order], cf[order])

Plots 5 and 6

Power read by the analyzer, and why it dips

What I did

The first plot is the marker height from the same narrow-span reading as plot 1. One reading per point.

For the second I took the 18 drift passes and plotted the mean, with bars from lowest to highest reading at each voltage.

Two different things show up:

  • One-off drops. 15 of 648 readings were more than 2 dB under their own median. They hit random voltages at random times. The mixer output did not move with them (0.23 mV at most), and the mixer is very sensitive to power. So the VCO did not really lose power. The analyzer missed the peak.
  • The broad low patch, 850 to 890 MHz. It repeats every pass. It also sits exactly where the VCO is steepest (correlation with tuning slope is −0.89).

My best guess, not proven

I read the peak through a 30 kHz filter. Any noise on the tuning pin wobbles the frequency. Where the VCO is steep, the same noise wobbles it further, less of the signal sits inside the filter, and the peak reads low.

\[ \Delta f_\text{rms} = K_v\,\sigma_{V_t}, \qquad K_v \approx 33\ \text{to}\ 90\ \text{MHz/V} \]

With \(\sigma_{V_t}\) of even 1 mV that is 33 to 90 kHz, wider than the filter. The fix is a 1 MHz filter and averaging. I have not run it: the analyzer was already off.

Step response

I drove the tuning pin with a 50 kHz square wave and watched the mixer output on the scope. The mixer output is slow enough that the scope sees it directly.

Plots 7 and 8

Rising and falling steps, five sizes overlaid

What I did

Each colour is one square wave, from 0.2 V up to a different top level. I took 3 long captures of each. Every capture holds about 140 rising and 140 falling edges.

I found every edge on CH1, lined them all up on the moment the drive crossed half-way, and averaged. About 420 edges per curve. Averaging is what makes them this smooth.

Top panel is the drive as measured. Middle is the mixer voltage as measured. Bottom is the middle panel put through the lookup.

These are raw averaged recordings. Nothing is divided out here. The spike at the start of the drive is the Syscomp, and the mixer is reacting to that shape.

Math

With \(t_k\) the time of edge \(k\) and \(N \approx 420\):

\[ \bar{y}(\tau) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} y(t_k + \tau) \]

Noise drops by \(\sqrt{N} \approx 20\).

Code: finding and stacking the edges 
dlo, dhi = an.levels(vd)                      # low and high level of the drive
edges = an.rising_edges(t, vd, dlo, dhi)      # 50 % crossings, interpolated

def stack_edges(tp, p, edges, grid):
    acc, n = np.zeros(len(grid), complex), 0
    for e in edges:
        x = (grid + e - tp[0]) / dt           # where each grid time falls in the record
        i0 = np.floor(x).astype(int); w = x - i0
        acc += p[i0] * (1 - w) + p[i0 + 1] * w
        n += 1
    return acc, n

Plot 12

Deconvolution: dividing the drive’s shape out

What I did

First I undid the curve. I ran each mixer trace backwards through the calibration to get the tuning voltage it corresponds to. Now input and output are both in volts and the system has a gain of 1.

Then I differentiated both, so each step becomes a pulse, took the Fourier transform, and divided output by input. I combined all 20 steps into one estimate.

To get a step response back I multiplied by a smooth 7 MHz low-pass, inverse transformed, and integrated.

What it says: flat to within 1 dB up to 3 MHz, a delay of 26 ns, and no slowing I can detect. The red deconvolved step rises in 68 ns, and an ideal instant system through the same 7 MHz filter also gives 68 ns.

Math

\[ H(f) = \frac{\sum_i X_i'^{*}(f)\, Y_i'(f)}{\sum_i |X_i'(f)|^2} \]
\[ s(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} \mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left\{ \frac{\sum_i X_i'^{*} Y_i'}{\sum_i |X_i'|^2 + \lambda}\; G(f) \right\} dt' \]

\(X_i'\), \(Y_i'\) are transforms of the differentiated drive and output for step \(i\). \(\lambda\) is 2 % of the DC value and stops the division blowing up. \(G\) is a Gaussian, −3 dB at 7 MHz.

Delay comes from the phase slope below 5 MHz: \(\tau = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{df}\).

Why only 7 MHz: the Syscomp’s edge has almost no energy near 10 MHz (the spike and the main rise cancel there). Dividing by nearly zero gives the tall fake peak in the top-left panel. Everything in the grey region is not real. Also, CH1 sits part-way along the cable, so what it sees is not exactly what the VCO pin sees. That is probably why the gain creeps above 0 dB.

Earlier test: bare VCO straight into the scope

Before the mixer was wired in, I had only the scope on the VCO’s RF output. The scope is 100 MHz and the VCO is near 800 MHz, so this needed a trick.

Plots 14 to 16

Frequency vs time, rise time, and settled frequency

What I did

The scope samples at 500 MSa/s. An 800 MHz signal shows up as a fake lower tone, an alias, near 200 MHz. The alias moves one-for-one with the real frequency, so I tracked the alias and converted back.

The signal was tiny, tens of microvolts, well under the scope’s noise. So I did not track single shots. I stacked 1112 edges per step. The VCO’s phase is random from edge to edge, so I could not average the waveform. I averaged a quantity that does not care about phase.

Result: the frequency follows the drive voltage with 0 to 12 ns of lag. Same story as the mixer test.

Math

Alias, and how to undo it for this band:

\[ f_\text{alias} = |f - k f_s|, \qquad f = 2 f_s - f_\text{alias}\ \ \text{for}\ 750 < f < 1000\ \text{MHz},\ f_s = 500\ \text{MSa/s} \]

The phase-proof quantity: multiply the filtered signal \(z\) by its own conjugate \(m\) samples earlier, average over edges, take the angle.

\[ p[n] = z[n+m]\, z^{*}[n], \qquad f_\text{alias} = \frac{f_s}{2\pi m}\,\arg \big\langle p[n] \big\rangle_\text{edges} \]
Do not trust the “static DC” curve in the third plot. It was taken before I found the Syscomp leak (below). The step results are fine.
Code: lag product and unfolding 
z = np.fft.ifft(2 * spec * w)                 # band-limited analytic signal
p = z[lag:] * np.conj(z[:-lag])               # phase cancels, frequency stays

def lag_to_alias(p, fs, band_start, lag):
    ph = np.angle(p * np.exp(-2j * np.pi * band_start * lag / fs))
    return band_start + np.mod(ph, 2 * np.pi) * fs / (2 * np.pi * lag)

def unfold(f_alias, fs, f_expected):
    zone = int(f_expected // (fs / 2))
    return zone * fs / 2 + f_alias if zone % 2 == 0 else (zone + 1) * fs / 2 - f_alias

What went wrong along the way

The Syscomp leaks. With a waveform selected and amplitude set to zero, it still puts about 100 mV peak-to-peak of that waveform on the output. My first two “DC” sweeps had the VCO hopping between two frequencies 3.4 MHz apart. I caught it in the noise test, switched the waveform off, and redid the sweep. Everything above uses the clean run.
I read the analyzer too early. At first I grabbed the marker before the sweep had finished. Power jumped by 15 dB between neighbours. I blamed the open tee port for that, and I was wrong. Fixed by taking one complete single sweep per reading.
