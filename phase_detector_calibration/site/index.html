Unit conversions used
\[ P_\text{dBm} = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{P_\text{mW}}{1\ \text{mW}}\right) \qquad P_\text{mW} = 10^{P_\text{dBm}/10} \qquad V_\text{rms} = \sqrt{P \times 50\ \Omega} \]

Example: 50 mW = 0.050 W. V rms = √(0.050 × 50) = 1.58 V. In dBm: 10 log10(50) = 17.0 dBm.

Datasheet. Connectors: SMA female in and out. Internal regulator, reverse-voltage protected.

Expected behavior
  • Draws about 80 mA at 6 V.
  • Adds 22 dB at 9.3–9.5 GHz, flat within ±0.6 dB.
  • Weak radar: −50 dBm in gives −28 dBm out.
  • Strong radar: −10 dBm in gives +12 dBm out. This is below the +17 dBm compression point.

Datasheet. Ports: LO = 2, RF = 3, IF = 1. The datasheet conversion loss is measured at a 30 MHz IF. Ours is 1.0–1.2 GHz, so expect some difference.

Expected behavior
  • IF frequency = RF − 8.3 GHz. 9.3 GHz gives 1.0 GHz. 9.5 GHz gives 1.2 GHz.
  • Output is about 7 to 8 dB below the RF input.
  • Weak radar: −28 dBm in gives about −36 dBm out.
  • Strong radar: +12 dBm in. This is above the +5 dBm compression point, so the output will be less than +4 dBm and distorted. It is below the +17 dBm damage limit.
  • LO leaks to the IF port 17 dB down: +10 − 17 = −7 dBm at 8.3 GHz. For a weak radar this is 29 dB stronger than the wanted signal.
  • LO leaks to the RF port 35 dB down: −25 dBm at 8.3 GHz going back toward the LNA.
  • A signal at 7.1–7.3 GHz gives the same IF as 9.3–9.5 GHz. The drawing has no filter before this mixer.
\[ f_\text{IF} = f_\text{RF} - f_\text{LO} \qquad P_\text{IF} = P_\text{RF} - L_c \qquad f_\text{image} = 2 f_\text{LO} - f_\text{RF} \]

Result

  • Delay 26 ns.
  • The step response of the part alone rises in 68 ns. A perfect part measured the same way also gives 68 ns. So the part is faster than this measurement can show.

How it was made

  1. The recorded mixer output is the response of the part to the Syscomp edge, which is slow and has a spike. The aim is the response to a perfect step.
  2. First remove the curve of the look up table: convert each mixer trace back to the tuning voltage that gives that mixer voltage. Now input and output are both in volts and the gain is 1.
  3. Differentiate the drive and the output. A step becomes a pulse.
  4. Take the Fourier transform of both.
  5. Divide the output transform by the drive transform. This undoes the convolution and gives the transfer function of the part. All 20 steps are combined in the division.
  6. Multiply by a 7 MHz low-pass filter, inverse transform, and integrate. This gives the step response of the part alone.
  7. Bottom right panel: grey is the recorded drive, blue is the recorded output (the raw system response), red is the step response of the part with the drive divided out, dotted is a perfect part through the same 7 MHz filter.
\[ H(f) = \frac{\sum_i X_i'^{*}(f)\, Y_i'(f)}{\sum_i |X_i'(f)|^2} \qquad s(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} \mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left\{ \frac{\sum_i X_i'^{*} Y_i'}{\sum_i |X_i'|^2 + \lambda}\, G(f) \right\} dt' \]

X′ and Y′ are the transforms of the differentiated drive and output. λ is 2 % of the value at zero frequency. G is the 7 MHz low-pass. Delay is the slope of the phase below 5 MHz.

How it was made

  1. The VCO output was connected to the scope. The mixer was not used.
  2. The scope samples at 500 MSa/s. A 765–875 MHz signal appears in the samples as a lower frequency, 235–125 MHz. This lower frequency is called the alias.
  3. The alias moves by 1 MHz when the real frequency moves by 1 MHz. So the real frequency is 1000 MHz minus the alias.
  4. The Syscomp drove a 50 kHz square wave from 0.5 V to a high level. Ten high levels from 0.75 to 3.0 V.
  5. The signal at the scope was tens of microvolts, smaller than the scope noise. One edge is not enough to see it.
  6. The VCO phase is different on every edge, so the waveforms cannot be averaged directly. Instead, each waveform is multiplied by a copy of itself a few samples earlier. The phase cancels in this product and the frequency remains.
  7. The product was averaged over 1112 edges per step. The angle of the average gives the alias frequency at each instant.
  8. Rise time is the time for the frequency to go from 10 % to 90 % of the step.

How to use it

  1. Read the mixer voltage.
  2. Find the two rows on either side of it.
  3. Draw a straight line between them and read the frequency.
\[ f(V) = f_i + (V - V_i)\,\frac{f_{i+1} - f_i}{V_{i+1} - V_i} \]
Code
use = cal[cal["vt_meas_v"] <= 1.2]             # the part where voltage only rises with frequency
mhz, mv = use["vco_freq_hz"] / 1e6, use["mixer_out_v"] * 1e3
order = np.argsort(mv)

def mv_to_mhz(x):
    return np.interp(x, mv[order], mhz[order])
